Fermentation & timing

Dough ferments in two separate stages that both matter across every style in this atlas: bulk fermentation (the whole mass rising after mixing) and ball/pan proof — staglio for a ball — after dividing and shaping. The right yeast amount depends entirely on how long you plan to wait between the two.

MethodYeast levelTotal timeBest for
Same-dayHigh — 1.5–3% fresh / 0.5–1% dry4–8h room tempNo planning ahead
Next-dayMedium — 0.3–0.6% fresh / 0.1–0.2% dry18–24h (fridge or room temp)Best everyday balance
Long cold fermentLow — 0.05–0.15% fresh48–72h fridge + 3–4h to finishMaximum flavour
PoolishTiny in preferment, little/none after8–16h preferment + 2–6h finalFlavour boost, no live culture
BigaLow-moderate in preferment16–24h cool + short final fermentStrong, elastic, deeper flavour
SourdoughWild culture, 15–30% starterVaries a lot — trust the doughMost complex flavour

Temperature is your dial

Yeast activity roughly doubles for every 8–10°C (15–18°F) rise. A fridge (~4°C/39°F) nearly stops it, letting you stretch fermentation over days without over-proofing. Pull dough out of the fridge 1.5–2h before shaping.

Reading the dough

  • Ready: puffy and jiggly, holds a light finger-dent that springs back about halfway.
  • Under-proofed: tight, springs back fully and immediately.
  • Over-proofed: slack, sticky, tears rather than stretches.
Time on a recipe card is a guess. Use the Calculator's water-temperature tool to hit a consistent dough temp so a given schedule means the same thing every time.

Flour & hydration

Flour strength sets a ceiling on how much water it can hold without the dough collapsing. Italian "00"/"0" flour is rated by W (alveograph strength); American styles instead specify flour by protein % — different measurement traditions for a related idea.

"00", "0", "1" — a grind, not a strength

The tipo number describes milling fineness and bran removal, not gluten strength. A bag can be tipo 00 and either weak or very strong — the number alone doesn't tell you.

W and protein %

W measures the energy needed to inflate a bubble of dough until it bursts — the best single predictor of how a dough handles water and time. American "bread flour" or "high-gluten flour" labelling (11–14% protein) serves a similar purpose for the styles that use it.

HydrationRecommended WFerment lengthTypical flour
45–60%200–260Same-day to next-dayStandard pizza flour
60–68%260–30018–24hMedium-strong flour / bread flour
68–80%300–33024–48hStrong flour / high-gluten blends
80%+330–400+24–72h+, often with a prefermentHigh-strength blends, biga/poolish essential

P/L — tenacity vs. extensibility

Lower P/L (roughly 0.5–0.6) means a more extensible dough that relaxes and stretches instead of springing back — generally preferable for hand-shaped styles.

The practical rule: if a bag doesn't state W or protein %, and you're not sure it's a dedicated pizza/bread flour, keep hydration modest and the ferment short. Save high hydration and multi-day ferments for a flour with real stated strength.

Method, step by step

  1. Mise en place. Weigh everything. Use the water-temperature tool to hit a consistent dough temp regardless of season.
  2. Mix. Dissolve yeast into the water first. Combine most of the water with the flour, work in salt, then stream in fat last once the dough is coming together.
  3. Optional autolyse. Rest flour and water together 20–30 minutes before adding salt/yeast/fat — hydrates the flour and jump-starts gluten with less kneading.
  4. Knead until smooth and elastic (see Kneading).
  5. Bulk ferment, covered airtight, until roughly doubled.
  6. Divide & shape. Ball for round styles, or press directly into an oiled pan for pan styles.
  7. Proof per the fermentation table, then shape/stretch/dimple per the style's own technique — see Style Guides.
  8. Top and bake per the style's own order and timing — this varies more between styles than almost anything else.
Sources: Gemignani, The Pizza Bible · Vetri & Joachim, Mastering Pizza · AVPN Disciplinare. Full list →

Kneading best practices

By hand

For high-hydration dough (65%+), slap and fold beats push-and-fold: lift, slap down, fold over, rotate, repeat 8–10 minutes. Develops gluten without extra bench flour quietly raising your hydration back down. Very stiff low-hydration doughs (Chicago, Roman Tonda) instead knead more like a bread dough or laminated pastry — see that style's own guide.

By machine

Low speed with a spiral or dough hook. Mix until the dough clears the bowl and feels smooth, 6–10 minutes. Over-mixing raises dough temperature via friction (see the water-temperature tool).

The windowpane test

Stretch a small piece thin between your fingers — a thin, translucent sheet without tearing means gluten development is sufficient.

Common mistakes: too much bench flour on a sticky dough; kneading well past windowpane; starting with too-warm water or over-mixing by machine.
Sources: Forkish, The Elements of Pizza · Myhrvold & Migoya, Modernist Pizza. Full list →

Sauce, across styles

Classic Neapolitan and Roman sauces are uncooked: hand-crush whole peeled tomatoes, salt, maybe oregano/basil, a drizzle of oil. American styles (NY, Sicilian, Chicago) more often use a lightly cooked, sometimes chunky, sauce, and Detroit applies it in raw cold stripes after baking.

Skip passata

Pre-made "pizza sauce" or passata is smoother and often pre-seasoned or reduced, throwing off both texture and balance. Hand-crushed or coarsely blended whole tomatoes keep texture and don't oversaturate the dough.

Drain the excess water

Let crushed tomatoes sit in a sieve for a few minutes, especially for longer, cooler home-oven bakes — excess liquid becomes steam and softens the crust.

Quantity and placement are where styles diverge most — see each Style Guide for specifics (amount, whether it's applied before or after baking, and what it's layered against).

Baking science

Oven typeTypical floor tempBake time
Wood-fired (masonry)430–480°C / 800–900°F60–90 seconds
Outdoor gas / pellet~450–500°C / 840–930°F60–90 seconds
Home oven + steel/stone250–300°C / 480–570°F max5–25 minutes depending on style

Steel vs. stone

Steel conducts heat far faster than stone — usually a better bottom crust and more browning in a home oven that can't reach wood-fired temperatures. Stone closes the gap at genuinely high-temp ovens.

Preheat longer than feels necessary

A stone, steel, or pan needs real time to saturate with heat — 45–60 minutes at maximum oven temperature is standard advice, not overkill.

Hotter and faster reads more "Neapolitan"; cooler and longer moves toward American pan-style territory. Neither is wrong — see each Style Guide for the specific target.

Sources & further reading

Nothing on this site's ranges, defaults, or explanations should be taken as coming from nowhere. The Atlas synthesizes one official standard, several well-regarded books, established test kitchens, and one long-running technical community, then adapts them for home kitchens. Each Wiki page and Style Guide links back to the specific sources behind it; this page is the full list.

The one official standard

The Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana (AVPN) publishes the only legally recognised specification for "Verace Pizza Napoletana," registered as an EU Traditional Speciality Guaranteed (TSG) under Regulation No. 97/2010: tipo 00/0 flour (W 220–380), 55–62% hydration, 50–55g salt/L water, 200–280g dough balls, wood-fired oven at ~485°C for 60–90 seconds.

Every other style in this Atlas is a home-kitchen adaptation of community and published-book consensus, not a single certifying body's spec — American regional styles in particular vary pizzeria to pizzeria far more than Neapolitan does. Treat each Style Guide's numbers as a well-triangulated starting point, not gospel.
